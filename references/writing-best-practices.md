# Revising a draft in the author's voice

This is the editor's brief for composing and for the last pass before delivery.
It serves the author's voice; it is not a house style to lay over it. When it
disagrees with the request, the reader or the author's own samples, they win.
Nothing below is a banned-word list. Each item names something worth a second
look, and the second look often leaves the sentence exactly as it was.

## Settle what is fixed first

Before touching a sentence, sort the draft into what you may rewrite and what
you may not. Quotations, names, dates, figures, formulas, product and field
labels, defined terms, citations and any wording the user said must stay are
fixed. So is anything a reader will look up rather than read, such as a table,
a form or a list of steps. Edit around them.

Facts get the same protection. A revision may not add a number, source, owner,
deadline, cause or degree of confidence the draft did not already support, and
it may not quietly drop one. "If the vendor signs by Friday" is part of the
claim; cutting it to save words changes what the sentence promises. Keep an
opinion an opinion and keep real doubt visible. Making a claim sound firmer than
its evidence is the costliest edit there is, because the author has to defend it.

## Work from the whole piece down

Read all of it before changing any of it. Most weak sentences are symptoms of a
weak shape, and fixing them one at a time polishes prose that should have been
moved or cut.

Then ask what the reader came for, and put that near the top when the form
allows. An email asking for approval should ask early; a status update should
say what changed before explaining why. Size the structure to the job: three
sentences answer a quick question, and a policy may honestly need ten pages.
Headings should say what the section concludes, not only what it covers.
Bullets suit items that really are parallel; an argument chopped into bullets
loses the words that joined it.

Cut the scaffolding readers skip: openings that announce what is coming,
paragraphs that restate the last one, transitions that connect nothing, closings
that sum up what was said a moment ago. Keep a repeat that serves someone. A
summary on top and the full account below may state the same recommendation for
two kinds of reader, and a sentence that marks the edge of scope stops a reader
assuming too much.

## Find where the draft goes soft

Soft prose gestures at a point without making it. "We made good progress on
alignment" could close any meeting ever held. The fix is nearly always a noun
and a verb: who decided what, what changed, what it costs, what happens next.
If the draft cannot supply them, the gap is in the thinking, and polish will
only hide it. Flag the gap rather than invent the detail.

Words take the same test. Prefer a concrete subject and a verb that does
something to a stack of abstractions, and trim hedges until the doubt left
matches the doubt that is real. Words like "leverage", "robust", "seamless" and
"landscape" turn up far more in generated text than in anyone's own writing;
when several gather in one paragraph, check that each names something. None is
forbidden. A technical, legal or clinical term that is exactly right for its
reader stays, glossed if that reader needs it; a plainer synonym that is less
exact is a worse word. "This change" should point at one thing the reader can
name without scrolling back.

Some illustrations. Each "after" assumes its facts are true; never borrow a
fact for the sake of a better sentence.

- "We are currently in the process of reviewing the proposal" becomes "We are
  reviewing the proposal."
- "The migration unlocks significant efficiencies" becomes "After the
  migration, the nightly job takes forty minutes instead of three hours."
- "Leadership has aligned on a path forward" becomes "Priya approved the
  smaller launch because the full one needed two engineers we don't have."

## Flourishes and the author's habits

Generated prose leans on a small kit of effects: the reveal built as "it's not
X, it's Y", the triplet slogan, the one-line paragraph that lands like a drum
hit, the metaphor stretched past its fit, unearned praise, sympathy that could
be pasted into any reply, unnamed "experts", the closing thought about the road
ahead. Any of them can work once. Used by reflex, they make prose sound
performed. When a flourish carries no claim of its own, state the claim plainly
and drop the effect.

Punctuation and layout are tools of the same kind. A colon, a dash, a bold
lead-in or a parallel list earns its place when it shows how two things relate
or helps the reader scan, and grates when it is there for emphasis every time.
A true contrast ("the bug is in the parser, not the network") is just accurate.

Here the samples arbitrate. If the author reaches for dashes, jokes, lowercase
openings or a signature aside, that is their voice, not a tell; the baseline
profile described in [ai-tells.md](ai-tells.md) is what lets the final pass
tell the two apart. Keep what they do, at the rate they do it.

## Know when to leave it alone

The best edit is usually the smallest one that removes a real problem for the
reader. If you cannot say what the reader loses when a sentence stays, let it
stay, and give a debatable pattern the benefit of the doubt. The same goes for
rhythm: cutting every sentence to one clipped length swaps one tell for another,
and a long sentence that reads easily is not a defect.

These checks ask whether the writing works for its reader. They are not a test
of whether a person or a model wrote it, and nothing needs disguising.

## Last read

Read the revision once, straight through, as its intended reader would.

- Could that reader restate the main point, and any request, after one read?
- Did every fact, name, number, caveat and open item survive?
- Is anything now claimed more strongly than its evidence allows?
- Does it still sound like the author in the samples, rough edges included?
- Does it do what the user asked, at a length the form deserves?

Keep this pass to yourself. The user asked for the writing, not a record of the
edit, unless they ask to see one.
